What the Aged Care Act 2024 Means for Your Workforce Compliance

By Veriyo October 2025 8 min read
← Back to Veriyo

On 1 July 2024, Australia's aged care sector entered a new regulatory era. The Aged Care Act 2024 replaced the Aged Care Act 1997 and introduced the most significant overhaul of workforce obligations in a generation. For compliance managers and HR teams, the message is clear: the old approach of manual checks and spreadsheet tracking is no longer adequate.

This article breaks down what changed, what the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission (ACQSC) is looking for in audits, and what practical steps providers should take to keep their workforce compliance audit-ready.

What changed on 1 July 2024

The new Act moved aged care regulation from a compliance-focused framework to a rights-based model — centring the rights and dignity of older people rather than minimum service standards. For providers, this shift has direct implications for how workforce compliance is managed, documented, and demonstrated.

The key workforce changes under the new Act include:

  • New worker screening obligations — all aged care workers must now meet the requirements of the Aged Care Worker Screening check, expanding on the previous National Police Check requirement.
  • Strengthened Code of Conduct requirements — the Aged Care Code of Conduct now applies to all workers and governing body members, with mandatory acknowledgement records required.
  • Expanded approved provider obligations — providers are responsible not just for employees but for contractors, agency staff, and volunteers who deliver personal care.
  • Civil penalty provisions — the Act introduced civil penalties for serious breaches, with maximum penalties exceeding $1.5 million for the most severe failures.
  • New governance requirements — boards and governing bodies must demonstrate active oversight of compliance, not just operational sign-off.
Important: The Aged Care Worker Screening check rollout is staged. Providers should check the Department of Health and Aged Care's current implementation timeline for their worker cohort, as requirements are phasing in across 2024–2026.

Standard 7: the most-failed standard in Australia

The Aged Care Quality Standards were updated alongside the new Act. Standard 7 — Human Resources remains one of the most consistently failed standards across Australian aged care homes.

1 in 5 Australian aged care homes failed to meet Quality Standards in Q3 2024–25, with HR and workforce the most commonly cited area. Source: ACQSC

Standard 7 requires that providers:

  • Have a sufficient number of appropriately skilled and qualified workers
  • Conduct thorough screening and recruitment checks
  • Provide ongoing training and development
  • Maintain accurate records of worker qualifications, clearances, and mandatory training
  • Respond promptly to performance concerns or conduct issues

The ACQSC's audit approach has become increasingly documentation-focused. Assessors now ask to see credential records, acknowledgement logs, and training completion data — and they expect to see them for every worker, not just a sample.

What auditors are looking for: Assessors will typically ask to see a complete list of current workers, their screening check dates and expiry dates, their qualification records, and evidence that they have acknowledged mandatory policies such as the Aged Care Code of Conduct. Being unable to produce these records on request is itself a finding.

The credential fraud problem providers don't talk about

In 2024–2025, the Australian Skills Quality Authority (ASQA) cancelled more than 3,500 aged care, disability, and childcare qualifications issued by rogue training providers. These were qualifications that appeared legitimate on paper — Certificate III in Individual Support, Certificate IV in Ageing Support — but were granted without students completing the required training.

Providers who hired workers holding these credentials without independently verifying them were left in an extremely difficult position: they had workers on shift who were legally unqualified, and no defensible record that they had done their due diligence.

Under the Aged Care Act 2024, the standard has shifted. Providers are now expected to independently verify qualifications — not simply accept a copy of a certificate at face value. This means checking against the training organisation's records or ASQA's national register, not just filing what the worker provides.

Contractors and agency staff: the compliance gap nobody talks about

One of the most common compliance oversights in aged care is the assumption that someone else has checked the contractor's credentials. Providers assume the agency has done it. The agency assumes the provider will verify on site. The result is that cleaning contractors, maintenance staff, allied health locums, and agency personal carers regularly enter aged care facilities with credentials that have never been independently verified by the provider.

Under the Aged Care Act 2024 and the updated Quality Standards, this is the provider's responsibility — not the agency's. If a contractor enters your facility and their police check is expired, or they hold a disqualified qualification, the provider is accountable.

Best practice: Maintain a contractor register that records the name, role, screening check details, and qualification status of every third-party worker who enters your facility — even for a single visit. This should be separate from your employee records and updated each time a contractor returns.

What providers should do now

1. Audit your current credential records

Pull a complete list of every active worker — employees, casuals, contractors, and agency staff — and check which credential records you actually hold on file. For many providers, the answer is uncomfortable: records are scattered across email threads, shared drives, and physical filing cabinets, and expiry dates are not being tracked proactively.

2. Identify credential gaps before the auditor does

Cross-check your worker list against the credentials required for each role. A Registered Nurse has different requirements than a Personal Carer or an allied health locum. Map the requirements by role, then identify which workers have gaps. Prioritise expired credentials and workers in direct care roles.

3. Establish a policy acknowledgement log

The Aged Care Code of Conduct requires workers to acknowledge the code. The same applies to your internal policies — Mandatory Reporting, Infection Control, Incident Reporting. Many providers have workers sign an acknowledgement at induction and never revisit it. When a policy is updated, the acknowledgement should be re-collected. Maintain a dated log of every acknowledgement, by worker and by policy version.

4. Create an audit-ready summary

When an ACQSC assessor arrives — announced or unannounced — you should be able to produce a workforce compliance summary within minutes, not hours. This summary should cover every worker's credential status, screening check expiry dates, and outstanding compliance items. If this would currently take your team hours to pull together, that is itself a risk.

5. Set expiry date alerts

Police checks, NDIS Worker Screening clearances, First Aid certificates, and Working With Children Checks all have expiry dates. A worker who was fully compliant at onboarding may have credentials that have since lapsed — and unless someone is actively tracking this, the organisation won't know until the credential is already expired. Build a system that alerts you 60–90 days before any credential expires.

The bottom line

The Aged Care Act 2024 raised the bar — on penalties, on provider accountability, and on the documentation standard ACQSC assessors expect. The providers who will manage this well are not the ones with the most compliance staff. They are the ones with the best systems.

Manual tracking — spreadsheets, shared drives, email chains — creates the gaps that audit findings are made of. The good news is that these gaps are entirely preventable with the right tools and processes in place.
